Inside AegirThe Earth on your lock screen is not a photograph

Apple ships the Astronomy wallpaper as a ray-traced globe — a cube-mapped planet lit in real time, wrapped in three shells of real cloud cover downloaded from Apple's servers, with your own position pulsing on the surface. Here is what came out of the firmware.

Finding it

Nothing in the firmware is labelled “Earth wallpaper.” The trail runs through codenames. Apple's poster extensions are named after gods and flowers — Celosia is the iOS 27 default, Fluidity is the iOS 18 one — and the Astronomy wallpaper turns out to be Ægir, a Norse sea god.

Two strings in AegirProxyApp.app confirmed it beyond doubt. Its display name is Astronomy, and it asks for location with a sentence that describes the wallpaper exactly:

NSLocationWhenInUseUsageDescription

“Your location is used to display your position on the globe.”

The same engine drives the Apple Watch Astronomy face, which is why it lives in a framework called NanoUniverse — “Nano” being Apple's long-standing internal prefix for watch software. One renderer, two products.

The planet is a cube

Earth ships as 24 texture tiles, and the arrangement gives the trick away: six groups of four. It is a cube map — the planet's surface projected onto the six faces of a cube, each face built from a 2×2 grid of 1024-pixel tiles, so every face is effectively 2048×2048.

Cube maps avoid the pinching and wasted resolution you get at the poles with a flat rectangular world map. Unwrapped, the six faces are immediately recognisable.

Every tile is ASTC-compressed with a full mipmap chain, and the layers are not all the same resolution — Apple spends detail where the eye actually looks. Surface colour gets the most; clouds get the least, because they are soft anyway.

Earth is the only body that gets all four. Mercury, the Moon and Mars ship colour and relief. The gas giants ship colour alone — there is no solid surface to catch a shadow. Saturn gets one extra file, a 384-pixel strip: its rings.

City lights are a separate layer

The night side is its own texture, and it is packed cleverly. The three colour channels hold the lights themselves — in colour, so sodium-orange streetlights and white LED districts read differently. The alpha channel holds a completely separate thing: a land-and-water mask with river networks drawn into it.

That mask is what lets the shader tint continents and oceans independently. The compiled shader carries four uniforms for exactly this — continentDayColor, continentNightColor, oceanDayColor and oceanNightColor — so land and sea can be graded separately on each side of the terminator.

It is ray-traced, not modelled

This is the part worth knowing. There is no sphere mesh. The renderer's own method name is _renderRaytraceSpheroid:, and the vertex format backs it up — NUNIAegirVrt_P2Vertex is just a pair of 16-bit integers, enough for the corners of a flat quad and nothing more.

Apple draws a rectangle over the screen and, for every pixel, fires a ray into it and works out mathematically where that ray strikes the planet. The uniforms are the giveaway: they are all squared radii, the arithmetic of ray–sphere intersection.

And there is not one sphere but six concentric shells, each of which the ray is tested against in turn:

  1. SurfacefloorRadiusSqr — the solid planet, sampled for colour, relief and city lights.
  2. Low cloudcloudLoRadiusSqr — the lowest weather deck.
  3. Mid cloudcloudMdRadiusSqr — offset outward by cloudAltitude.
  4. High cloudcloudHiRadiusSqr — the top deck, thinnest and faintest.
  5. Inner atmosphereatmosRadiusInner — where the blue haze begins.
  6. Outer atmosphereatmosRadiusOuter — the glowing rim you see against black space.

Because the clouds sit at genuine altitude above the surface rather than being painted onto it, they cast real shadows and slide with a slight parallax as the globe turns. The shader has dedicated controls for the shadowing: earthCloudShadowStrength, and a soft ramp between earthCloudShadowEaseFrom and earthCloudShadowEaseTo.

The full tuning surface

The compiled renderer exposes a struct of 35 constants named NUNIAegirPipelineConstants. It reads like a lighting rig:

  • Sun — distance, equatorial radius, glow radius, exposure floor, and a full RGB colour.
  • Surface — earthLightPower, ambient strength, and specular power, strength and breakup, which stops the ocean acting as a perfect mirror.
  • Illumination — RGB and strength for the city lights, so the night side can be warmed independently.
  • Atmosphere — RGB, strength, glow falloff, and a soft terminator ramp so day does not snap into night.
  • Bloom — radius, strength and threshold, applied in a dedicated offscreen pass.

The frame is assembled across three offscreen buffers — scene, bloom, then post — before it reaches the screen.

The clouds are real, and they expire

The strongest find in the teardown is a class called AegirCloudCoverService. The Earth wallpaper does not use canned weather. It downloads current cloud cover from Apple and re-skins the planet with it.

The service fetches three files — cloudLevelLow, cloudLevelMid and cloudLevelHigh — matching the three cloud shells the shader intersects, one texture per altitude deck. A file converter then combines and transcodes them for the GPU. The error and log strings left in the binary spell out the whole lifecycle:

So the weather on your lock screen has a shelf life. When it lapses, the wallpaper posts an expiry notification and goes back for a fresh set. The globe you glance at is, within the limits of that refresh, showing you the actual sky.

You are on it

That location permission is spent on a single element: NUNIAstronomyLocationDot, a small view with an inner and outer disc that pulses — it exposes pulseDuration and pulseAlphaDelay, and the renderer has a dedicated pass, _renderLocationDot:, plus its own pair of shaders. It is the same beacon idiom as the blue dot in Maps, transplanted onto a planet.

There is also a NUNIAstronomyRotationModel, and its vocabulary is revealing: push:, isPulling, and isAtHomeCoordinate. The globe can be spun, and it knows how to return home — home being where you are standing.

One last detail from the poster's rendering configuration: motion effects are switched off, but the depth effect is left on. No gyroscope parallax — the globe is already three-dimensional and does not need faking — but the clock is still allowed to tuck behind the Earth's limb.

How this was read

Apple publishes beta firmware on an open CDN, and the whole chain turned out to be walkable without a device or a developer account.

  1. Locate the buildiOS 27.0 beta 5 for iPhone 16 Pro — a 12.3 GB restore image on updates.cdn-apple.com.
  2. Read it without downloading itAn IPSW is a ZIP, and the CDN honours byte ranges. Reading the archive's index off the tail exposed all 133 members for about 24 MB of traffic.
  3. Unlock the filesystemThe root filesystem is an Apple Encrypted Archive. Its header names a key URL on wkms-public.apple.com — Apple serves the unwrapping key openly.
  4. Mount itDecrypted to a 9.5 GB APFS volume that macOS mounts natively, read-only.
  5. Recover the codeFramework binaries live in the dyld shared cache inside a second encrypted volume. Extracting NanoUniverse from it yielded the class list, method names and uniform structs quoted throughout.

What this does not cover. The Aegir poster is delivered on demand, so the extension binary itself is a placeholder in the firmware; everything above comes from the shared renderer that ships in the OS, plus the shipped textures and shaders. Shader logic was read from compiled metadata — uniform names, texture bindings and entry points — not from decompiled arithmetic. Numeric values of the 35 tuning constants live in the poster payload, not the framework, so the structure is known and the settings are not.
